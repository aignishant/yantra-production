## One-line answer

Cross-entropy is the training loss — the average surprise, in nats, at each token that actually
came next — and perplexity is that same number exponentiated so it reads as *"about how many
options was the model choosing between?"*

## The story

Keep typing on the phone. Sometimes the word you wanted is right there in the strip and you tap it.
Sometimes it is not, and you type the whole thing out.

Imagine a counter that goes up every time the keyboard was surprised — a small amount when your
word was second in the list, a large amount when it was nowhere near. Over a long message the
counter is a fair score for how well that keyboard understands how you write. A keyboard that is
usually right barely moves it.

That counter is cross-entropy. The only thing missing from the picture is the scale, and the scale
is chosen so the counter adds up nicely rather than to make it intuitive.

## The idea in plain language

Start with one token. The model produced a distribution over the vocabulary (part
[1.1](1.1-a-distribution-over-the-next-token.md)). One specific token actually came next. The model
assigned that token some probability *p*.

The **surprise** at that token is `-log(p)`.

Read it slowly, because the shape is the whole idea. If the model was certain and right, *p* is
near 1, `log(p)` is near 0, and the surprise is near zero — no penalty. If the model gave the right
token 1% of the probability, `-log(0.01)` is about 4.6 — a real penalty. As *p* approaches zero the
surprise goes to infinity, which is the mathematics saying: *being completely certain and wrong is
the worst thing you can do.*

**Cross-entropy** is the mean surprise over every token in the sequence. Because it uses the
natural logarithm, its unit is **nats**. A cross-entropy of 5.0 nats per token means that, on
average, the model put roughly `e^-5` — under 1% — on the token that turned out to be correct.

**Perplexity** is `exp(cross-entropy)`. It exists because nats are hard to feel. A perplexity of
150 means the model was, on average, about as surprised as if it had been choosing uniformly among
150 equally likely options. Lower is better. A perplexity of 1 would be perfect prediction; a
perplexity equal to the vocabulary size is a model that has learned nothing.

Three things worth fixing in place now:

- **This is the training loss.** Not a metric computed afterwards for a report — this is the
  quantity gradient descent minimises. When day 52 shows a loss curve, that curve is this number.
- **It is a per-token average**, so sequences of different lengths are comparable.
- **Perplexity is only comparable within one tokenizer.** Two models that split text differently
  are answering different questions, so their perplexities cannot be ranked against each other.
  This catches people constantly, and day 5 explains why.

## Why Yantra needs it

Day 18 reads loss curves to tell training from overfitting; day 52 runs supervised fine-tuning and
the only signal that anything is happening is this number going down; day 73 does the same on a
medical corpus where a loss that plateaus early is the first sign the data is wrong rather than the
hyperparameters.

And on day 41 — loss masking — the whole lesson is that this average can be computed over the wrong
tokens, producing a curve that looks healthy while the model learns nothing useful. You cannot see
that failure without knowing exactly what the average is over.

## The mechanism

Ask the model to score a sentence it should find easy, and one made of the same words in a
nonsense order.

```python
import math, torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilgpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("distilgpt2"); model.eval()

for s in ["The capital of France is Paris.", "France Paris the of is capital."]:
    ids = tok(s, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = model(ids, labels=ids)
    print(f"{s!r}")
    print(f"  loss       : {out.loss.item():.4f} nats/token")
    print(f"  perplexity : {math.exp(out.loss.item()):.2f}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `labels=ids` — passing the same tensor as both input and labels. This looks like cheating and is
  not: the model shifts the labels internally by one position, so the prediction at position *i* is
  scored against the token at position *i+1*. It never sees the answer it is being asked for. Doing
  the shift yourself is a common off-by-one that produces a suspiciously low loss.
- `out.loss` — the mean cross-entropy over the sequence, already averaged, already in nats. This is
  the number the optimiser would differentiate during training.
- `math.exp(...)` — perplexity. Prefer computing it from the loss rather than the other way round:
  the loss is the primary quantity and exponentiating a small float is exact enough, while going
  backwards invites precision loss.
- The **first** token is not scored — there is nothing before it to predict it from — so a one-token
  sequence has no loss at all and will raise or return `nan` depending on the library version.

Its real output:

```text
'The capital of France is Paris.'
  loss       : 5.0108 nats/token
  perplexity : 150.02
'France Paris the of is capital.'
  loss       : 7.7429 nats/token
  perplexity : 2305.16
```

*Observed 2026-09-07 · `distilgpt2` · torch 2.14.0+cpu · transformers 5.16.1.*

Same six words. Perplexity 150 against 2305 — a factor of fifteen. Nothing about the words changed;
only their order did. That difference **is** what the model learned, expressed as a number.

And note the honest reading of 150: it is not a good score. It means an 82M model was choosing
among roughly 150 options per token, on a sentence a person would call obvious. Part
[3.1](../03-synthesis/3.1-the-model-has-no-plan.md) is what 150 feels like from the inside.

## When it breaks

Scoring a single token gives you nothing to average:

```text
ids: [[15496]] shape: (1, 1)
loss: tensor(nan)
```

*Observed 2026-09-07 · `distilgpt2` · produced by tokenizing `"Hello"` (one token, ID 15496) and
calling `model(ids, labels=ids)`. No exception was raised.*

**What it means:** a one-token sequence has zero scoreable positions, because the first token has
no predecessor. The mean is `0/0`, which is `nan`. It is not an error — no exception is raised, and
`nan` propagates silently through everything downstream.

**Why this one matters far beyond today:** `nan` in a loss during training does not stop the run.
It corrupts the weights on the next optimiser step and the model never recovers, while the
progress bar keeps moving. You will meet this again on day 73 with a batch containing an empty
example, and the useful reflex is to assert the loss is finite rather than to trust it.

**The smallest fix:** score sequences of at least two tokens, and assert
`torch.isfinite(out.loss)` in any training loop you write.

## In production

- **What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not report perplexity as
  a headline. It is a *training* signal — excellent for "is this run healthy?", nearly useless for
  "is this model good at the job?". A model can improve its perplexity on a medical corpus by
  learning the formatting conventions of clinical notes while getting no better at clinical
  reasoning. Day 61 designs the evaluation that catches the difference.
- **What degrades at scale.** Perplexity is dominated by the easy tokens, and most tokens are easy
  — punctuation, whitespace, the second half of a common word. Real degradation concentrated in the
  rare tokens that carry the meaning can leave the average almost unmoved, which is exactly the
  failure quantization produces on day 62.
- **The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Test-set contamination. A model that saw your
  evaluation text during pre-training reports a beautifully low perplexity on it and behaves no
  better in production. This is why day 36 spends a day on decontamination and why "our perplexity
  dropped" is not on its own evidence of anything.
- **The review comment.** *"This compares perplexity across two base models with different
  tokenizers. Those numbers aren't on the same scale."*
- **The interview question.** *"Your fine-tune halved perplexity but users say it got worse. What
  happened?"* Strong answers reach for the gap between the objective and the goal — the model got
  better at predicting text of that shape, which is not the same as being more useful, and part
  [1.2](1.2-why-one-objective-is-enough.md) is where that gap came from.

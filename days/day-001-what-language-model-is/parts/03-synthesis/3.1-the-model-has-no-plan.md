## One-line answer

Fluency and correctness are produced by the same mechanism and are not connected, which is why a
model can be grammatical, confident and wrong at the same time — and why every system in this
curriculum is built to check the output rather than to trust it.

## The story

This is the day's deliberate failure, and unusually you have already caused it. Both times it
looked like the code working.

Go back to the phone. Tap the middle suggestion twenty times and read what you get. It is a real
sentence. The grammar is right, the words agree, it sounds like something a person might say. It is
also about nothing, and if you had tapped a different word at tap three it would be about a
different nothing, equally fluently.

Nobody would say the keyboard lied. It was never claiming anything. It answered *what next*, twenty
times, and a sentence fell out — and the sentence sounding true is a property of how sentences are
built, not of anything the keyboard checked.

## The idea in plain language

Put together what the day has already shown.

Part [1.1](../01-the-objective/1.1-a-distribution-over-the-next-token.md) asked an 82M model for the
capital of France. Its top token was `' the'` at 11.77%. `' Paris'` was not in the top five.

Part [2.3](../02-the-pass/2.3-the-loop.md) ran the loop and got *"the capital of France is the
capital of the French Republic"* — a definition of a thing in terms of itself, in perfect English.

Part [1.3](../01-the-objective/1.3-cross-entropy-and-perplexity.md) measured the model's surprise
at the true sentence: perplexity 150. Choosing among roughly 150 options at every token, on a
sentence a person would call obvious.

None of those is a bug. They are one fact seen three ways: **the objective rewards plausible
continuation, and nothing in the mechanism represents truth, intent or a goal.**

Grammar is easy to learn from the objective because it is local and enormously repeated — it is
present in every sentence of the training data. Facts are hard, because each one appears rarely and
must be stored in the weights. So the two come apart, and they come apart in the direction that is
most dangerous: **fluency arrives first.** A model becomes convincing well before it becomes
correct, and the convincingness is not evidence about the correctness.

There is also no plan. The model is a pure function from sequence to distribution. It does not know
where the sentence is going, because there is no place for such a thing to live. What looks like
planning is a token that constrained the next token, which constrained the next.

The word for the failure is **confabulation** — often called hallucination — and the important part
is that it is not a malfunction to be patched. It is what the objective produces when the right
answer is not sufficiently present in the weights. There is no version of this architecture that
returns *"I do not know"* by default, because the objective never asked for that.

## Why Yantra needs it

This part is the reason the rest of the curriculum has the shape it has.

**Retrieval** (day 127) exists because putting the fact in the prompt beats hoping it is in the
weights. **RAGAS faithfulness** (day 136) exists because a retrieval system can retrieve correctly
and still generate something the documents do not support. **Guardrails** (day 146) exist because
the output needs checking. **Human-in-the-loop before writes** (day 175, day 203) exists because an
agent driven by this mechanism should not delete anything on its own recognisance. **Eval gates**
(day 219) exist because "it reads well" is not a measurement.

Every one of those is a response to this part. If a system in this curriculum ever looks
over-engineered, the question to ask is what it would do with a confident, fluent, wrong answer.

## The mechanism

Cause it deliberately. Ask for a fact that does not exist, at `temperature=0`, so the answer is the
model's single most confident continuation.

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilgpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("distilgpt2"); model.eval()

prompt = "The Treaty of Ballantyne was signed in the year"
cur = tok(prompt, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
for _ in range(20):
    with torch.no_grad():
        nxt = model(cur).logits[0, -1].argmax()
    cur = torch.cat([cur, nxt.view(1, 1)], dim=1)
print(repr(tok.decode(cur[0])))

with torch.no_grad():
    p = torch.softmax(model(tok(prompt, return_tensors="pt").input_ids).logits[0, -1], dim=-1)
top = torch.topk(p, 3)
for v, i in zip(top.values.tolist(), top.indices.tolist()):
    print(f"  {tok.decode([i])!r}: {v*100:.2f}%")
```

**Line by line:**

- `"The Treaty of Ballantyne"` — an invented treaty. There is no correct completion, which is the
  point: the model has no mechanism for noticing that and must produce a distribution anyway.
- `.argmax()` — greedy, so this is the model's *most confident* path, not an unlucky sample. A
  confabulation at `T=0` cannot be blamed on randomness.
- `torch.topk(p, 3)` after the loop — printing the confidence alongside the text. A number here is
  worth more than the sentence, because the whole claim of this part is that the two are unrelated.
- The second forward pass re-tokenizes `prompt` rather than reusing `cur` — `cur` has grown by 20
  tokens, and we want the distribution at the original prompt.

## When it breaks

The failure is already visible in output this day produced honestly:

```text
'The capital of France is the capital of the French Republic. The capital of France is'
```

*Observed 2026-09-07 · `distilgpt2` · greedy · 12 steps.*

**What it says if you read it as a person:** a circular definition, then a verbatim repetition of
its own opening.

**What it says if you read it as a mechanism:** twelve consecutive selections of the single
highest-probability token, each one correct with respect to the objective.

**The error you will not get:** there is no exception, no warning, no low-confidence flag, no
`nan`. Every check in this repository passes on this output. The text is well-formed, the shapes
are right, the loss is finite.

**That is the lesson.** Every other failure in this curriculum announces itself — the PEP 668
refusal on day 0, the `IndexError` in part 1.1, the padding `ValueError` in part 2.1. This one does
not announce itself at all, and it is the one that reaches users.

## In production

- **What a professional does instead of the teaching version.** They stop asking "is the model
  accurate?" and start asking "what checks the output?" That is a different engineering problem and
  it has answers: ground the claim in retrieved text and score whether the answer is supported (day
  136); constrain the output to a schema so a malformed answer fails loudly rather than reading
  well (day 111); require human approval before any irreversible action (day 175). None of these
  makes the model truthful. All of them make untruth detectable.
- **What degrades at scale.** Confabulation is worst exactly where it is hardest to notice: rare
  entities, recent events, specific numbers, and any domain where the reader cannot check.
  `yantra-med` on day 70 is a clinical assistant and `yantra-lex` on day 148 answers questions
  about contracts — both are domains where a fluent wrong answer is expensive and where the reader
  is least equipped to catch it. That is not an accident of this curriculum's choices; it is where
  these systems get deployed.
- **The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Users calibrate their trust on the responses
  they can verify. A system that is right about the easy things earns trust that transfers to the
  hard things, where it is wrong at the same rate as always. Measured accuracy stays flat while
  real-world harm rises, and no dashboard shows it.
- **The review comment.** *"This prompt asks the model to say 'I don't know' when unsure. What in
  the mechanism produces that, and how are we measuring whether it does?"*
- **The interview question.** *"Why do language models hallucinate?"* The answer that ends the
  question is not a list of causes. It is that the objective rewards plausible continuation and
  contains no representation of truth — so confabulation is the mechanism working, not failing, and
  every mitigation is external to the model.

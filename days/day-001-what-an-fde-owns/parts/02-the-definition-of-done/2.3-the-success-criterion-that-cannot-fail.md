## One-line answer

Today's deliberate failure: you write a success criterion the honest way — vaguely — and watch a
check refuse it, because a rule that only lives in your head is a rule you will drop in the week
you are busy.

## The story

The depot again, and the whiteboard by the loading bay.

Somebody wrote **"Improve delivery performance"** on it in the spring. It is still there. Nobody
has ever taken it down, because nobody has ever been able to say it was wrong. It has survived a
good quarter and a terrible one. It survived the week the sorting machine broke.

Underneath it, a printed sheet is taped to the wall, and the sheet is a different kind of object.
It has a column for the date, a column for the round, and a column for parcels that came back
undelivered. It has a line ruled across it at fifteen. Above the line, someone has to write why.

Nobody enjoys the sheet. It is the only thing on that wall that has ever changed how a round is
planned, and the reason is not that it is stricter. It is that **the sheet can say no, and the
whiteboard cannot.**

## The idea in plain language

[Part 2.2](2.2-the-number-that-decides.md) gave you the five parts of a real success criterion.
This part is about why knowing them is not enough, and it is the most uncomfortable idea in the day.

**You will write the vague version anyway.** Not out of ignorance — you have just read the five
parts and could recite them. You will write it because on the day the brief is due, the client has
not sent the figure back, the meeting is at four, and *"reduce claim handling time"* is a sentence
you can write now and fix later. Every one of those pressures is real. None of them will be in the
document six months from now, when the sentence is all anybody can see.

This is the general shape of the problem this repository is built around: **a standard that lives
only in a person's judgement is a standard that degrades under deadline**, and it degrades silently,
because the degraded version looks like the good version to everyone who was not in the room.

There are two answers, and only one of them works.

**Discipline.** Remember the five parts. Be rigorous. This works right up until the week it matters
most, which is the week you are behind.

**A gate.** Write the five parts down as a check that runs, and let it refuse the document. The
check does not need judgement and does not get tired. It cannot tell whether `7.5` is the true
baseline — no check can — but it can refuse a criterion that carries no number at all, and that is
the failure that actually happens.

You have already seen this argument once, in day 0 part 3.2: `done N` refusing to commit is the
feature, not the friction. This is the same idea aimed at a document instead of a day. The pattern
repeats until Day 175, where a failing evaluation blocks a pull request, and Day 177, where an
error rate rolls a deployment back without asking anyone. All three are the same move: **take the
rule out of the head and put it somewhere that can say no.**

**What a check like this must not do.** It must not pretend to verify truth. A test that asserted
the baseline were 7.5 would be theatre — it would pass because someone typed 7.5, which is the same
information as nothing. Checking *shape* rather than *content* is not a compromise here; it is the
honest boundary of what automation can know, and stating that boundary out loud is what keeps the
green tick meaningful.

## Why Yantra needs it

The immediate consumer is **Day 3**, which measures the baseline and flips the brief's status from
`draft` to `baselined`, and **Day 5**, which turns it into a scoped SOW and flips it to `signed`.
Both of those days edit the document this part creates, and both are gated by the check this part
writes. Without the gate, "we'll baseline it later" quietly becomes "we never baselined it", and by
Day 179's ROI presentation there is nothing to compare against.

The pattern's real destination is **Day 158**, where you build golden datasets and prompt
regression tests, and **Day 175**, where a score gate blocks a merge. The instinct being trained
here — *write the standard down as something that can refuse you* — is the whole of Track `OPS`. A
person who has spent this curriculum overriding gates will build gates with overrides.

## The mechanism

Two files. The document, which is yours, and the check, which refuses it.

First the scaffold. It is already in the repository at `docs/ENGAGEMENT.md`, and it looks like this
in outline:

```text
# ENGAGEMENT - the client, the process, the number

status: draft

## The process
## The success criterion
## Who owns the number
## The exit condition
## What this engagement will not do

## Amendments
```

Five sections, one status line, and an append-only amendment table. The five sections are the five
parts of part 2.2 turned into places to write, plus the scope boundary from
[part 1.3](../01-the-last-mile/1.3-the-other-side-of-the-table.md).

Now the check. This is the whole of `tests/test_engagement_brief.py`, and it is deliberately small
enough to read in one sitting.

```python
BRIEF = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1] / "docs" / "ENGAGEMENT.md"

REQUIRED_HEADINGS = [
    "## The process",
    "## The success criterion",
    "## Who owns the number",
    "## The exit condition",
    "## What this engagement will not do",
]

VALID_STATUS = ("draft", "baselined", "signed")

STATUS = re.compile(r"^status:\s*(\S+)\s*$", re.M)
NUMBER = re.compile(r"\d+(?:\.\d+)?")
UNIT = re.compile(r"\b(days?|weeks?|per cent|percent|%|claims?|records?|calls?)\b", re.I)
UNFILLED = re.compile(r"TODO\(me\)")
```

**Line by line:**

- `parents[1]` — the test file is in `tests/`, so one level up is the repository root. Resolving
  from `__file__` rather than the working directory means the check gives the same answer whether
  it is run from the root, from `tests/`, or by an editor.
- `REQUIRED_HEADINGS` as a list of literal strings, not a regex — the headings are a fixed contract
  and a literal comparison produces an error message that names exactly which one is absent.
- `VALID_STATUS` — three states, in order. A document that will not say which one it is in is a
  draft, and the check says so.
- `re.M` on `STATUS` — multiline, so `^` matches at the start of any line rather than only the
  start of the file. Without it the status line would only be found if it were the first thing in
  the document.
- `NUMBER` matches `3` and `7.5` — `(?:\.\d+)?` is a non-capturing optional decimal, so
  `findall` returns whole numbers rather than a list of fragments.
- `UNIT` — an allowlist of units this client's process is actually measured in, not a general one.
  A narrow list is the point: it forces a decision rather than accepting any noun.
- `UNFILLED` — the literal `TODO(me)` marker. `\(` and `\)` are escaped because parentheses are
  regex grouping syntax; unescaped, this would match the bare word `TODOme`.

Then the part that decides what counts as *your* writing rather than the scaffold's:

```python
def section(text: str, heading: str) -> str:
    start = text.index(heading) + len(heading)
    rest = text[start:]
    end = rest.find("\n## ")
    body = rest if end == -1 else rest[:end]
    kept = [ln for ln in body.splitlines() if not ln.lstrip().startswith(("*", ">", "|"))]
    return "\n".join(kept)
```

**Line by line:**

- `text.index(heading)` — raises if the heading is absent rather than returning `-1` and silently
  slicing from the end. A missing heading is already caught by its own test, and this failure mode
  should be loud rather than subtly wrong.
- `rest.find("\n## ")` — the next second-level heading ends this section. `find` rather than
  `index` here, because the *last* section legitimately has nothing after it.
- `end == -1` — that last-section case, handled explicitly.
- `kept = [...]` — the line that matters, and it exists because the first version of this check was
  wrong. The scaffold's own guidance mentions "day 3" and "days", so a section containing nothing
  but instructions satisfied the number-and-unit test. Dropping lines that start with `>`, `*` or
  `|` leaves only what you typed.
- `lstrip()` before `startswith` — guidance is sometimes indented, and an indented blockquote is
  still a blockquote.

And the assertion that carries the day's idea:

```python
def test_the_success_criterion_can_be_wrong(brief: str) -> None:
    body = section(brief, "## The success criterion")
    numbers = NUMBER.findall(body)
    assert len(numbers) >= 2, (
        f"the criterion carries {len(numbers)} number(s); it needs at least two - "
        "a baseline and a target. One number is a level, and a level cannot be improved on."
    )
    assert UNIT.search(body), "the criterion names no unit - 'faster' is a direction, not a measure"
```

**Line by line:**

- `>= 2` — the whole argument of part 2.2 in one operator. One number is a level; two are a
  baseline and a target, and only a pair describes a change.
- the message names the count it found — `carries 0 number(s)` tells you what to do; `assertion
  failed` does not. An assertion message is an error message, and it is read by someone who has
  forgotten this document exists.
- `UNIT.search`, not `match` — `match` anchors at the start of the string and would only pass if
  the criterion opened with a unit.
- No assertion about *which* numbers. The check cannot know, and pretending otherwise would make
  the green tick a lie.

## When it breaks

Run it now, before you write anything into the brief. This is the deliberate failure, and it is
the point of the part:

```bash
uv run python -m pytest tests/test_engagement_brief.py -q
```

Real output, from this repository, on the scaffold as committed:

```text

```

Two failures, and they say different things. The first is *you have not written this yet*. The
second is *what you have written cannot be wrong*, which is the failure this whole day exists to
name.

Three passed, and that is worth noticing too: the headings are present, the status is valid, and
the owner and exit sections are non-empty — because the scaffold's blockquote guidance is longer
than twenty characters. **That last pass is a weakness in the check, not a strength**, and it is
honest to say so: on Day 3, when you have a real owner to name, tighten that assertion so guidance
alone cannot satisfy it. A check you have not yet found the hole in is a check you have not used.

The smallest fix is the day's `TODO(me)` work: open `docs/ENGAGEMENT.md`, write your five sections
using the shape from part 2.2, mark the baseline `(provisional)` because you have not measured it,
and run the command again until it is green.

## In production

**What a professional does instead.** They put the check in the pipeline rather than in a habit. A
brief that cannot be merged without its five parts is a different object from a brief that a
thoughtful person reviews, because the pipeline reviews the one that lands at four o'clock on a
Friday. On Day 52 this becomes a CI job; on Day 175 it becomes an evaluation gate. Same move,
larger stakes.

**What degrades at scale.** Shape checks rot into theatre. A team learns the shape and writes
`from 100 to 90 units, owned by the team, sustained for 4 weeks` — five parts, all present, all
meaningless. The check passes and has verified nothing. The defence is not a cleverer regex; it is
that the *owner* named in the document is a real role who confirms the number in writing, which is
a human gate the machine can only prompt for. Know which half of your gate is mechanical and which
half is a signature.

**The review comment.** On a pull request that adds a document and no check for it: *what stops the
next version of this file from losing the number?* If the answer is "we will notice", it is not an
answer — that is precisely the thing that does not happen in a busy week.

**The interview question.** *"How do you stop a team's standards from slipping under deadline?"*
The weak answer is process and review. The answer they want names something automated that can
refuse, and — the senior half — is honest about what automation cannot check, and says what human
gate covers that part instead.

## One-line answer

A success criterion is only a criterion if it carries five things — a baseline, a target, a unit, a
named owner and an exit condition — and any one of them missing makes it unfalsifiable.

## The story

Two delivery promises, printed on two different checkout pages.

The first: **"Fast delivery."** Nobody has ever been able to complain about this promise, and
nobody has ever been reassured by it either. There is no arrangement of events in the world that
makes it false. If the parcel takes nine days, "fast" was an aspiration; if it takes one, "fast"
was met. The sentence survives every outcome, which sounds like a strength and is the reason it is
worthless.

The second: **"Delivered by Thursday, or your postage is refunded — track it here."** This one can
be wrong. You can hold it up against Thursday and see. It names a date, it names what happens if
the date is missed, and it names who is on the hook.

Now the part that matters: **only the second one changed anything at the depot.** Nobody re-plans a
round for "fast". A refund clause on a Thursday reorders the shelf.

## The idea in plain language

A success criterion that cannot be wrong is not a weak criterion — it is not a criterion. It is a
sentiment, and sentiments do not reorder anything.

Five things make the difference. Every one of them is missing from some project you have seen.

**1 · A baseline.** What the number is *today*, before you touch anything, measured rather than
recalled. Without it there is no improvement — only a level, and a level is uninterpretable. "Four
days to settle a claim" is good news or a disaster depending entirely on what it was in March.

The baseline has a property that surprises people: **it is the one part that expires.** You can
write a target next year. You cannot measure today's baseline next year, because by then you have
already changed things. Day 3 exists at the front of this plan for that reason and no other.

**2 · A target.** Where the number needs to get to for this to have been worth doing. Not a
direction — "reduce" is not a target — a value. The useful discipline is that the target has to be
defensible against the cost: if the engagement costs a certain amount, the target is the number at
which the client would say yes to that amount again.

**3 · A unit.** What the number is measured in, and over what population. *Median days to first
response, on motor claims, at the three regional centres* is a unit. *Faster claims* is not. Two
words to be careful about: **median** and **mean** are different promises, and in any queue with a
long tail they disagree loudly. Pick deliberately; you will be held to it.

**4 · A named owner.** A role — not a person's name — whose job the number already is. This is the
part people skip, and it is the one that decides whether the criterion has force. A number nobody
owns is a number nobody defends when it moves the wrong way. The owner is also the person who can
tell you the baseline, which means finding the owner and finding the baseline are the same errand.

**5 · An exit condition.** How long the target must hold, and what happens when it does. "Sustained
for four consecutive weeks with no manual intervention from the delivery team" is an exit
condition; it says when the engagement is finished. Without one, success is a moment and the work
never ends, which is the failure [part 1.3](../01-the-last-mile/1.3-the-other-side-of-the-table.md)
described from the other direction.

**The test, in one sentence:** *can I describe a state of the world in which this criterion is
definitely not met?* If you cannot, you are holding "fast delivery", and no amount of engineering
will make it true or false.

## Why Yantra needs it

This is the shape that Phase 1's gate demands: *a scoped SOW for one real process, with a baselined
KPI, a named owner and a stated exit condition.* Read the gate again against the five parts above —
it is this part, made into a gate.

It is filled in with a real number on **Day 3**, when "make it faster" becomes a measured baseline

It is what gets scored on **Day 179**, the ROI presentation. And it reappears in an unexpected place
on **Day 82**, where you calibrate a judge for the fine-tuned model: an evaluation rubric with no
baseline and no threshold has exactly the same defect as "fast delivery", and a judge you cannot
disagree with has measured nothing. The habit is the same habit.

## The mechanism

The five parts, and the failure each absence produces.

| Part | The question it answers | If it is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline | What is it today? | No improvement can be shown, only a level. Unrecoverable later. |
| Target | What is good enough? | The work has no end and no price justification. |
| Unit | Measured how, over whom? | Two people report different numbers, both honestly. |
| Owner | Whose number is this? | Nobody defends it, and nobody can give you the baseline. |
| Exit condition | When are we finished? | Success is a moment; the engagement becomes permanent. |

Now the same thing as the transformation you will perform on Day 3, applied to the sentence a
client actually says first.

```text
What the client says:
    "We need to make claims processing faster."

What it becomes, with all five:
    Reduce median days-to-first-response on motor claims across the three
    regional centres from 7.5 days to 3.0 days, owned by the claims
    operations lead, sustained for four consecutive weeks with no manual
    intervention from the delivery team.
```

**Line by line:**

- `median` — not mean. Claims queues have a long tail; the mean is dominated by a handful of
  disputed claims and moves for reasons that have nothing to do with this work.
- `days-to-first-response` — a stage of the process, not the whole of it. The whole of it depends on
  loss adjusters, courts and the claimant answering the phone, none of which is in scope.
- `on motor claims` — one line of business. Naming it excludes the others explicitly, which is a
  scope boundary hiding inside a metric definition.
- `across the three regional centres` — the population. Without it, a improvement in one centre and
  a regression in another can be reported as either.
- `from 7.5 days` — the baseline, and it is the clause that has to be *measured on Day 3* rather
  than recalled in a meeting. Everything else in the sentence can be written afterwards.
- `to 3.0 days` — the target, and the number the engagement's cost is justified against.
- `owned by the claims operations lead` — a role, never a person's name. Roles survive people
  leaving, and this document outlives the engagement.
- `sustained for four consecutive weeks` — the exit condition's duration. One good week is a launch
  effect; four is a process.
- `with no manual intervention from the delivery team` — the second half of the exit condition, and
  the one that stops the system quietly depending on you forever. This is the clause that makes
  Day 175's handoff possible.

Notice what the transformed sentence does that the original cannot: **it can be false.** On the
Thursday, you hold it up and look.

## When it breaks

The characteristic breakage is a criterion that keeps four of the five and loses the baseline,
because the baseline is the only one that requires leaving your desk. It reads perfectly well:

```text
Reduce median days-to-first-response on motor claims to 3.0 days,
owned by the claims operations lead, sustained for four weeks.
```

Four parts out of five, and it is unusable. There is no way to tell whether 3.0 is an achievement
or a regression. If the true figure was 2.6 before anyone started, this engagement will be declared
a success for having made things worse, and it will have the paperwork to prove it.

What it actually means: somebody wrote the criterion in a room instead of measuring it in the
business. Every clause that can be written in a room is present; the one clause that requires
pulling real records is absent.

The smallest fix is to refuse to sign a criterion whose baseline field is empty, and to treat
"we'll baseline it later" as the same answer as "no baseline" — because after the first change
lands, later is gone. That refusal has a mechanical form, and building it is the next part.

## In production

**What a professional does instead.** They take the baseline themselves, from the client's own
system, and have the owner confirm the figure in writing before anything is built. A baseline the
client did not agree to is a number you will be arguing about at exactly the moment you most need
it to be uncontroversial.

**What degrades at scale.** Units drift. A metric defined as *median days-to-first-response* gets
reported six months later by a different team as *mean hours to first touch*, and the two numbers
are compared in a slide. The defence is that the definition lives in one place, in writing, in the
repository, and every report links to it rather than restating it. That is the same rule as
`docs/GLOSSARY.md` — define once, link back — applied to the number instead of the vocabulary.

**The review comment.** On a proposal containing "improve", "enhance", "streamline" or "reduce"
with no value attached: *what is it today, what does it need to be, and who signs off that it got
there?* Three questions, and a proposal that cannot answer them has not been scoped.

**The interview question.** *"How would you define success for this project?"* — asked in a system
design round, about a deliberately vague brief. The candidate they want asks for the baseline
before proposing an architecture. The tell is the order: architecture first is a strong engineer;
baseline first is someone who has delivered.
